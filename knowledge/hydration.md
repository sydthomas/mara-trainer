# Hydration, sodium and heat

## Bottom line for the coach
- Start runs well hydrated: drink with normal meals through the day and, when needed, a few hours before the run so there is time to absorb it and pass the excess. For everyday drinks, choose water (still or sparkling) and unsweetened drinks. [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007] [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030]
- During long runs, aim to limit large fluid losses without drinking more than you lose; sweat rates vary a lot between people, so the plan should be individual and practiced on long runs. [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Women are at greater risk of exercise-associated hyponatremia (low blood sodium), linked to smaller body size, drinking too much water and longer race times. Warn against overdrinking plain water in the marathon. Symptoms and what to do: see health-referrals.md. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@wms-exercise-associated-hyponatremia-2019]
- Sweat-rate testing (weighing before and after a run) is something a sports dietitian can help with; the coach does not ask for or record weights. [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007]
- Sodium: the general-population limit is under 2,300 mg a day, but highly active people may benefit from more to replace sweat losses. Drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrate can beat plain water in some conditions. [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030] [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007]
- After runs, replace the fluid and sodium lost; how fast depends on how big the deficit is and how soon the next session comes. [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007]
- Heat: most heat illness is preventable by managing risk factors; heat stroke is an emergency (see health-referrals.md). For a hot race, 1 to 2 weeks of daily heat sessions improved women's heat tolerance and performance in the heat. [@acsm-exertional-heat-illness-2023] [@kelly-heat-adaptation-females-2023]

## What the sources agree on
- The 2007 ACSM stand sets the goal during exercise as preventing a water deficit of more than 2% of body mass, avoiding large electrolyte shifts, and using customized plans because sweat rate and sweat electrolyte content vary widely; it suggests weighing before and after exercise to estimate sweat loss (percent of body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007]
- Exercise-associated hyponatremia means blood sodium below 135 mmol/L during or up to 24 hours after prolonged activity. It can look like heat exhaustion or heat stroke, and misdiagnosis worsens outcomes, which is why field diagnosis belongs to medical staff. [@wms-exercise-associated-hyponatremia-2019] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Hyponatremia can come from sodium loss, too much fluid, or both. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Sodium and electrolytes are essential for hydration; highly processed, salty foods are still best limited in everyday eating. [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030]
- On long efforts (over about 90 minutes), a carbohydrate drink of about 6% taken at roughly 500 to 1000 mL per hour fits the 30 to 60 g per hour carbohydrate guideline, giving fluid and fuel together. Fueling detail: see fueling-during.md. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Exertional heat stroke can damage organs and kill; early recognition, stopping exercise and rapid whole-body cooling are essential. [@acsm-exertional-heat-illness-2023]
- Heat training in women (30 studies, 235 women, mean age 24) lowered resting core temperature by about 0.15 °C, exercise core temperature by about 0.4 °C and heart rate by about 14 beats per minute, raised sweat rate by about 30%, did not change plasma volume, and improved performance tests in the heat by about 39%. [@kelly-heat-adaptation-females-2023]
- Fuller heat adaptation in women came from about 8 to 14 days of daily sessions (around 65 minutes a day) at moderate-to-high intensity; about a week gave partial adaptation. A falling heart rate during heat sessions is a cheap sign it is working. [@kelly-heat-adaptation-females-2023]

## Where they differ
- Planned drinking vs avoiding overdrinking: the 2007 ACSM stand frames drinking during exercise around preventing a 2% loss with a planned, individual schedule, while the newer women's position stand points to excess water intake as a hyponatremia driver in women. The difference is emphasis and date (2007 vs 2023); the 2020 hyponatremia guideline's prevention advice could not be checked because only its abstract was read (see Gaps). [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@wms-exercise-associated-hyponatremia-2019]
- Luteal phase: one review suggests drinking more in the luteal phase and measuring sweat rate across the cycle, while the women's position stand notes that luteal progesterone reduces sodium retention and raises hyponatremia risk. Together: more fluid may be reasonable, but not plain water beyond thirst and losses. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Sex differences in sweating: women start sweating later and sweat less than men in the heat, but those differences largely disappear when men and women are matched for fitness, size, age and acclimatization; the women's position stand stresses instead that women have less body water to lose. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Sodium: the general dietary guidelines set a daily limit for the public but acknowledge highly active people may need more; they give no amount for athletes. [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030]

## Women-specific notes
- Women have less total body water than men (about 31 vs 44 liters, about 49% vs 58% of body mass) and less in the extracellular space, so the same fluid loss may hit harder, and heat and heart strain may appear at lower levels of dehydration. Plasma volume losses were similar between sexes under similar exercise. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Estrogen lowers the threshold for thirst and for the water-retaining hormone vasopressin, and this shift persists on oral contraceptives; thirst may vary across the cycle, which could raise hyponatremia risk in long races if intake doesn't match losses. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Core temperature is higher in the mid-luteal phase, yet only 7 of 30 heat-training studies scheduled testing by cycle phase; the heat-adaptation findings are women-only but not cycle-controlled. [@kelly-heat-adaptation-females-2023]
- Many hydration studies fail to control for the menstrual cycle. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- The 2007 fluid stand, the hyponatremia guideline and the heat-illness statement do not give sex-specific guidance in the parts read. [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007] [@wms-exercise-associated-hyponatremia-2019] [@acsm-exertional-heat-illness-2023]

## Gaps
- The 2007 ACSM fluid replacement stand is paywalled; only the abstract was read. Its specific fluid and sodium amounts and hyponatremia advice are not here.
- The Wilderness Medical Society hyponatremia guideline (2020) is paywalled; only the abstract was read. Its prevention advice (including drinking to thirst), risk factors and evidence grades are not here, so the drink-to-thirst vs drink-to-plan question is not settled from text actually read.
- The ACSM heat illness statement (2023) abstract says nothing about fluids or heat acclimatization; full text not read.
- The ACSM/Academy/Dietitians of Canada joint position stand (2016) hydration guidance is paywalled; abstract has no numbers.
- No approved source read gives sweat sodium values, an hourly fluid range for women runners, or sodium amounts for long runs.
- The eatright.org page had no hydration content.
- Sims, ROAR (sims-roar-revised-2024) is a book and was not read.
