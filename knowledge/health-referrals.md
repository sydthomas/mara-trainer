# Warning signs and referrals

## Bottom line for the coach
- The coach never diagnoses. Bone stress injuries are confirmed by a medical provider with imaging (X-ray or MRI), so the coach cannot rule one out; pain that could be bone, pain that persists, or pain that changes how she runs goes to a sports medicine doctor before more running. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024] [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Take bone stress risk seriously in women: women runners have about twice the bone stress injury risk of men, and a past bone stress injury is among the strongest risk factors for another. [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021] [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Collapse, confusion or acting strangely during or after a long run or race is an emergency: stop, get on-site medical help or call emergency services. Heat stroke needs fast whole-body cooling, and exercise-associated hyponatremia looks a lot like heat illness, so nobody should guess which one it is. [@acsm-exertional-heat-illness-2023] [@wms-exercise-associated-hyponatremia-2019]
- Missed or stopped periods, a bone stress injury, food restriction, guilt about food or pressure about weight: be supportive, give no numbers, and suggest a doctor plus a registered sports dietitian (and a mental health professional when eating is a worry). [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2] [@ioc-reds-consensus-2023]
- Unusual fatigue or a drop in performance without a training reason: suggest a doctor visit and blood test (iron status is one thing they may check); no self-prescribed iron. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- General load advice the coach may give: avoid sudden jumps in training load, and count life stress and travel as load too. That is load management, not treatment. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]
- After childbirth, refer to a pelvic health physiotherapist before running. [@goom-postnatal-return-to-running-2019]

## What the sources agree on
- Bone stress injuries are overuse injuries that are more common in women. Across 38 prospective studies (35,689 runners, 40.8% women), overall injury rates did not differ by sex, but women had roughly double the bone stress injury risk. [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021] [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Site matters. Injuries in the pelvis, sacrum and femoral neck heal slowly and can progress to a hidden fracture, and full return can take up to about 6 months; shin, fibula and most metatarsal injuries generally heal faster. The Triad consensus lists further high-risk sites (including the navicular, the front of the shin bone and the base of the fifth metatarsal) with high complication rates and delayed return. Deciding site and grade is a clinician's job. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024] [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Risk factors for bone stress injury from the sources read: a past bone stress injury, missed periods, low bone density, energy deficiency or disordered eating, and calcium and vitamin D intake. In one study of 45 women runners, high-risk-site injuries also went with more short sleep (80% slept under 7 hours on weeknights versus 33% of uninjured women). [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2] [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021] [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- After any bone stress injury, the study authors recommend a full risk review (nutrition and energy, periods, sleep, and bone density testing for high-risk sites), and the Triad consensus counts a current or past bone stress injury as a reason for a bone density scan. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024] [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Energy deficiency warning signs worth a referral (screening topics, not a diagnosis): changes in or loss of periods, a bone injury that cost training, special diets or food avoidance, worry about weight, compulsive exercise, and eating too little without meaning to. Return to training after these problems is decided by the physician with the care team. [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- REDs affects women and men, can harm both health and performance, and is assessed by clinicians with a dedicated tool that rates severity and risk and links them to training advice. [@ioc-reds-consensus-2023] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Heat illness. Exertional heat stroke is a medical emergency that can damage organs and kill; survival depends on early recognition, stopping exercise and rapid whole-body cooling, and outcome seems linked to how long the very high body temperature lasts. Most exertional heat illness is preventable by recognizing and changing known risk factors. [@acsm-exertional-heat-illness-2023]
- Hyponatremia. Exercise-associated hyponatremia is low blood sodium during or up to 24 hours after prolonged exercise. Its signs overlap with heat exhaustion and heat stroke, and mistaking one for the other is linked to worse outcomes, which is why the runner should get medical help rather than self-treat. [@wms-exercise-associated-hyponatremia-2019]
- Fluids. Sweat rates and sweat salt vary widely between people, so fluid plans should be individual rather than one-size-fits-all. [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007]
- Iron. Iron deficiency is common in women athletes (9 to 60% across studies) and linked to a small endurance loss; deficient athletes should see a sports medicine doctor or sports dietitian, and supplements should be supervised and comply with anti-doping rules. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- Load. The IOC defines load broadly: rapid changes in training or racing, crowded race schedules, psychological stress and travel; poor load management is linked to injury, and athletes should monitor training load, well-being and injury. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]

## Where they differ
- Is load the main cause of bone stress injury? The IOC statement ties poor load management to injury in general. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016] In women runners specifically, the bone stress study found that running load rates did not separate injured from uninjured women, while bone density, eating attitudes and sleep did; it was small and cross-sectional. Both matter: manage load, and refer when energy or period warning signs appear. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Fluids: individual plans versus overdrinking risk. The ACSM fluid stand (abstract) focuses on preventing too much dehydration and tailoring fluids to sweat rate; the WMS guideline focuses on hyponatremia. Only the abstracts were read, so this section does not settle how much to drink; see `hydration.md`. [@acsm-exercise-fluid-replacement-2007] [@wms-exercise-associated-hyponatremia-2019]

## Women-specific notes
- Women carry about twice the bone stress injury risk, and in women the risk ties closely to periods, bone density and energy, so a woman with a past bone stress injury or missing periods should be pointed to a doctor early rather than told to rest and see. [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021] [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024] [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Hormonal contraception hides whether her own cycle is normal, so "regular bleeds on the pill" does not rule out energy deficiency. [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- The ISSN female-athlete stand says hormones in the luteal phase raise hyponatremia risk; treat it as a reason for care, not a rule. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- The ACSM heat-illness statement gives no sex-specific recognition or cooling guidance. [@acsm-exertional-heat-illness-2023]

## Gaps
- No approved source read here lists bone stress injury symptoms (for example, where and when it hurts); the coach should not offer a symptom checklist and should refer on any pain that could be bone.
- The ACSM heat-illness statement, the WMS hyponatremia guideline, the ACSM fluid stand, the IOC load statement and the IOC REDs statement were read at abstract level only (full texts are paywalled). Specific symptom lists, cooling methods, sodium advice and return-to-running steps are therefore not summarized.
- Illness (colds, fever, stomach bugs) and when to train through it: no approved source read covers this. Refer to a doctor for anything more than mild symptoms.
- Chest pain, fainting, heart symptoms: not covered by sources read; these are emergencies or urgent doctor visits.
- Rehab protocols and graded return to running after injury are out of scope by design.
