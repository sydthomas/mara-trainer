# Recovery, sleep and rest

## Bottom line for the coach
- Aim for about 8 hours of sleep a night; the sleep review recommends roughly 8 hours for adults and notes that fewer than 6 hours over 4 or more nights impairs thinking, mood, metabolism, appetite and immunity. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Short sleep is worth flagging in a woman runner: in a study of 45 women runners, 80% of those with high-risk bone stress injuries slept under 7 hours on weeknights, versus 33% of uninjured women, and the authors recommend counseling on sleep for recovery and injury prevention when training increases. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Practical sleep habits from the review: a regular routine (early sessions can disrupt it), no TV in bed, limit caffeine, and a short nap if she slept badly or woke early. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Pre-race sleep is often poor (nerves, thoughts about the race); in one survey two thirds of athletes slept worse before at least one important competition, so a restless night before the race is common. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Rest days and easy weeks are part of load management: avoid sudden jumps in load, and count work stress, poor sleep and travel as load too. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]
- Sleep aids and supplements (melatonin, tart cherry, valerian) have weak or mixed evidence; the coach should not recommend them, and some herbal products carry anti-doping contamination risk. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]

## What the sources agree on
- Sleep loss and performance. Effects depend on the task: a night without sleep reduced distance covered in a treadmill run in one small study of men but did not change weightlifting, and partial sleep loss mainly hurt longer, submaximal efforts, with effects adding up over several nights. Mood worsened consistently after sleep loss. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Sleep extension. In a small study of six collegiate basketball players, extending sleep improved sprint times, shooting accuracy and mood; a conference abstract reported similar gains in swimmers. A 30-minute nap after a short night improved sprint times and alertness. These are small studies, mostly not in women and not in marathoners. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Athletes may sleep less than they think: one actigraphy study found athletes spent about 8.6 hours in bed but slept about 6.9 hours, with longer time to fall asleep than non-athletes. In a survey of 632 athletes, 66% slept worse than normal before at least one important competition, mostly from thinking about the race and nerves. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Sleep, illness and pain. Short sleep was linked to immune changes and more inflammation in general-population studies, and sleep loss may amplify pain; direct evidence in athletes is minimal and the review found no direct data linking sleep loss to injury risk. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Food and sleep. Evidence that specific foods improve sleep is limited and contradictory: high-carbohydrate meals gave mixed results, tryptophan showed some promise but not in athletes, tart cherry juice had limited support, melatonin and valerian showed weak or subjective-only effects. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Load and recovery. The IOC treats load as more than miles: rapid changes in training, crowded racing, psychological stress and travel all count, and it recommends monitoring training load, well-being and injury alongside prescribing training. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]

## Where they differ
- Does short sleep raise injury risk? The 2014 sleep review found no direct data linking sleep loss to injury. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014] The 2024 study of women runners found short sleep much more common in women with high-risk bone stress injuries and advises sleep counseling for injury prevention. The newer study is small, cross-sectional and self-reported, so it shows an association, not proof that sleep causes injury. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Pre-sleep protein. The ISSN female-athlete stand says pre-sleep protein may reduce soreness and help recovery in women, but notes the evidence is limited, it may matter mainly for women short on energy, and one study found a pre-sleep chocolate milk drink did not improve next-morning running in women athletes. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] Halson's earlier review found protein effects on sleep itself promising but untested in athletes. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]

## Women-specific notes
- Several studies (not all) report more sleep disturbance and poorer sleep in the luteal phase (the days before a period); if she notices this, logging sleep alongside her cycle can show whether it applies to her. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Poor sleep after hard sessions may slow glycogen refilling, muscle repair and thinking, according to the ISSN female-athlete stand. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Most sleep studies in the 2014 review either studied men or did not report sex; only a few studies of women are included, so sleep guidance here rests mostly on men and mixed groups. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]

## Gaps
- Recovery practices such as massage, foam rolling, compression, ice baths, cold-water immersion, stretching and active recovery: no approved source covers them, so the coach should say so rather than recommend them.
- How many rest days a week and when to take them: no approved source read gives a number; the plan guardrails in `plan_rules.py` set the structure.
- The IOC load statement was read at abstract level only (paywalled), so its specific monitoring tools and load-change limits are not summarized.
- The Halson review discloses honoraria from the Gatorade Sports Science Institute; much of its evidence is small, older, non-athlete or extreme sleep-deprivation studies.
- Recovery after the marathon itself: not covered by sources read.
- Overtraining and heart-rate variability monitoring: no approved source read.
