# Recovery, sleep and rest

## Bottom line for the coach
- Aim for about 8 hours of sleep a night; the sleep review recommends roughly 8 hours for adults and notes that fewer than 6 hours over 4 or more nights impairs thinking, mood, metabolism, appetite and immunity. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Short sleep is worth flagging in a woman runner: in a study of 45 women runners, 80% of those with high-risk bone stress injuries slept under 7 hours on weeknights, versus 33% of uninjured women, and the authors recommend counseling on sleep for recovery and injury prevention when training increases. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Practical sleep habits from the review: a regular routine (early sessions can disrupt it), no TV in bed, limit caffeine, and a short nap if she slept badly or woke early. Pre-race sleep is often poor (nerves, thoughts about the race); in one survey two thirds of athletes slept worse before at least one important competition, so a restless night before the race is common. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Rest days and easy weeks are part of load management: avoid sudden jumps in load, and count work stress, poor sleep and travel as load too. No single marker detects overtraining, and a dip in wearable heart rate variability (HRV) is not by itself fatigue: in women it tends to run lower late in the cycle, and it should add to training decisions, not make them. Performance that stays down with lasting fatigue despite rest goes to a doctor. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016] [@meeusen-overtraining-consensus-2013] [@dejager-wearable-hrv-menstrual-cycle-2026]
- Sleep aids and supplements (melatonin, tart cherry, valerian) have weak or mixed evidence; the coach should not recommend them, and some herbal products carry anti-doping contamination risk. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Don't promise that stretching after a run speeds recovery: compared with passive rest, it showed no effect on strength or next-day soreness in mostly male, small trials, and certainty was very low. Foam rolling may ease soreness a little, with limited evidence and no way to rule out placebo. Both are optional if she likes them. [@afonso-post-exercise-stretching-2021] [@wiewelhove-foam-rolling-2019]
- Cold-water immersion is optional. Against simply resting after one hard session, it eased soreness for a day or two and made people feel more recovered the next day, with a small gain in next-day power and none in strength or endurance performance. The studies are mostly of men, nobody could be blinded, and they don't show what regular use does to training gains. It was studied for recovery after hard exercise in healthy people: it is not a treatment for an injury or swelling, which go to a professional. Cold on a fresh injury is a separate question: a critical review (abstract only) found pain relief is its only shown benefit in people, with no human study showing it limits damage or speeds healing. Less pain after icing therefore says nothing about the injury, and it is never a reason to skip or delay getting it assessed. [@moore-cold-water-immersion-meta-2022] [@racinais-cryotherapy-soft-tissue-review-2024]
- Sauna after runs is optional, and the evidence is one weak study. University club runners (16 women and 10 men, about 19 to 20 years old) who sat in a hot dry sauna straight after easy or long runs for three weeks had a lower core temperature and heart rate in a hot-room run test and a higher VO2max afterwards, women and men alike. They chose their own group, about half of the recruits never finished, and the study was too small for a full comparison with controls by sex. Describe it as what one study did, not as a protocol for her. It studied healthy runners in training, not sauna with an injury or illness. [@kirby-sauna-sex-differences-runners-2021]

## What the sources agree on
- Sleep loss and performance. Effects depend on the task: a night without sleep reduced distance covered in a treadmill run in one small study of men but did not change weightlifting, and partial sleep loss mainly hurt longer, submaximal efforts, with effects adding up over several nights. Mood worsened consistently after sleep loss. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Sleep extension. In a small study of six collegiate basketball players, extending sleep improved sprint times, shooting accuracy and mood; a conference abstract reported similar gains in swimmers. A 30-minute nap after a short night improved sprint times and alertness. These are small studies, mostly not in women and not in marathoners. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Athletes may sleep less than they think: one actigraphy study found athletes spent about 8.6 hours in bed but slept about 6.9 hours, with longer time to fall asleep than non-athletes. In a survey of 632 athletes, 66% slept worse than normal before at least one important competition, mostly from thinking about the race and nerves. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Sleep, illness and pain. Short sleep was linked to immune changes and more inflammation in general-population studies, and sleep loss may amplify pain; direct evidence in athletes is minimal and the review found no direct data linking sleep loss to injury risk. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Food and sleep. Evidence that specific foods improve sleep is limited and contradictory: high-carbohydrate meals gave mixed results, tryptophan showed some promise but not in athletes, tart cherry juice had limited support, melatonin and valerian showed weak or subjective-only effects. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Load and recovery. The IOC treats load as more than miles: rapid changes in training, crowded racing, psychological stress and travel all count, and it recommends monitoring training load, well-being and injury alongside prescribing training. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]
- Post-exercise stretching. A meta-analysis of randomized trials (11 reviewed, 10 pooled, 229 participants; healthy, aged 17 to 38, mostly male, sedentary to athletes) found no effect versus passive recovery on strength (ES -0.08, 95% CI -0.54 to 0.39) or on soreness at 24, 48 or 72 hours (ES -0.09 to -0.24). Confidence in the evidence was very low, samples were small, and the authors advise against evidence-based recommendations of stretching for recovery. This says stretching isn't shown to speed recovery, not that it is harmful. [@afonso-post-exercise-stretching-2021]
- Foam rolling. A meta-analysis of 21 studies (454 participants, elite to untrained) found rolling after exercise reduced perceived muscle pain by about 6% (g = 0.47, small), while its small effects on sprint (+3.1%, p = 0.12) and strength (+3.9%, p = 0.28) were not statistically significant. Rolling before exercise gave a small short-term flexibility gain (+4.0%, g = 0.34) without impairing performance. Studies were small, pressure was rarely measured, and participants could not be blinded. [@wiewelhove-foam-rolling-2019]
- Cold-water immersion after hard exercise. A meta-analysis of 52 randomized studies in physically active people compared it with passive rest after a single hard session. After high-intensity exercise it lowered soreness at 1, 24 and 48 hours (SMD -1.10, -0.89 and -0.82), raised perceived recovery at 24 hours (SMD 0.66) and gave a small power gain at 24 hours (SMD 0.22, just significant) that was gone by 48 hours. After eccentric, muscle-damaging exercise the soreness benefit showed at 48 to 96 hours and power was better from 24 to 72 hours. Strength did not improve at any time point, and pooled endurance performance did not differ at 24 or 48 hours. [@moore-cold-water-immersion-meta-2022]
- Cold-water dose. Protocols ranged from 5 to 20 °C and from 3 to 30 minutes. Shorter and colder immersions went with larger effects only for a blood marker of muscle damage and, for duration, endurance performance; no dose effect showed for power or the other outcomes. The coach has no evidence-based dose to give. [@moore-cold-water-immersion-meta-2022]
- Limits of the cold-water evidence. Participants and researchers could not be blinded, so a placebo effect can't be ruled out. Results varied a lot between studies and few studies tested endurance. Only single sessions were included, so routine use across a training block is outside its scope. It studied healthy, uninjured people, so it says nothing about cold on an injury or swelling. [@moore-cold-water-immersion-meta-2022]
- Cold on a fresh soft tissue injury (critical review, abstract only). Clinicians often ice an injury to cut pain, limit further damage and help healing. The review's search found no human studies showing that cold limits secondary injury or improves tissue repair; pain relief is the one benefit shown in people. What is known about how it works comes largely from animal studies, some of which suggest cold may delay or impair repair, and the authors call its effect in injured people uncertain. Pending human studies, they suggest to clinicians that cold may be used in the first 6 hours after an injury for pain (and possibly bruising) and with caution beyond 12 hours. The coach does not pass that on as a treatment plan: cold may dull pain, it is not shown to treat the injury, and the injury still gets assessed. [@racinais-cryotherapy-soft-tissue-review-2024]
- Post-run sauna, what one study did. Trained middle-distance and cross-country runners from a university club kept their normal training; the sauna group also sat in a dry sauna (101 to 108 °C in the abstract; the methods give about 86 °C at chest height) for about 29 minutes, about 3 times a week for 3 weeks, mostly straight after easy or long runs, drinking water freely. In a 30-minute run in a 40 °C room afterwards, peak core temperature was 0.3 °C lower and peak heart rate 9 beats a minute lower, with no difference between the sexes in those changes. In cool-room tests VO2max rose about 5% in women and 3% in men (the abstract gives 5% for both together), and time to exhaustion and speed at a fixed lactate level also rose, again without a sex difference. Blood plasma volume did not change. The authors report that the control group did not improve on these tests. [@kirby-sauna-sex-differences-runners-2021]
- Post-run sauna, limits. Runners chose their own group, so it was not randomized. Of 80 recruited, 41 finished (26 sauna, 15 control); the paper lists injury and scheduling among the reasons. Because of the dropout, the main analysis looks at the sauna group alone, before and after, with the comparison against controls left to a supplement. Everyone took daily iron tablets during the study. Participants were young university runners, sessions ended at 30 minutes or when the runner could no longer tolerate it, and the text read gives no safety thresholds. It tested heat adaptation and fitness markers, not race results, soreness or recovery between sessions. [@kirby-sauna-sex-differences-runners-2021]
- HRV from wearables. A living systematic review of 16 studies (about 19,000 women, roughly 60% of them from a single study; some athletes, many of unstated activity level) found HRV higher early in the menstrual cycle and lower toward its end in naturally cycling women, most consistently for the time-domain measures. The studies differed too much in devices, timing and how phases were defined to pool, fewer than half (7 of 15) confirmed phase with hormone or ovulation tests while the rest estimated it by calendar, temperature or an app, and the paper gives different figures for the size of the change in different places, so no number is quoted here. [@dejager-wearable-hrv-menstrual-cycle-2026]
- Overreaching and overtraining. A joint European and American consensus (abstract only) describes overtraining as too much training load with too little recovery. Short-term (functional) overreaching can end in better performance once she recovers. Non-functional overreaching and overtraining syndrome are hard to tell apart, the syndrome is identified mainly by excluding other diseases and contributors such as poor nutrition and deficiencies, and none of the hormone, performance, mood, blood or immune markers reviewed meets the criteria to be a reliable test. [@meeusen-overtraining-consensus-2013]

## Where they differ
- Does short sleep raise injury risk? The 2014 sleep review found no direct data linking sleep loss to injury. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014] The 2024 study of women runners found short sleep much more common in women with high-risk bone stress injuries and advises sleep counseling for injury prevention. The newer study is small, cross-sectional and self-reported, so it shows an association, not proof that sleep causes injury. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Pre-sleep protein. The ISSN female-athlete stand says pre-sleep protein may reduce soreness and help recovery in women, but notes the evidence is limited, it may matter mainly for women short on energy, and one study found a pre-sleep chocolate milk drink did not improve next-morning running in women athletes. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] Halson's earlier review found protein effects on sleep itself promising but untested in athletes. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Soreness tools. The stretching meta-analysis found no soreness benefit, while the foam rolling one found a small benefit whose authors say the evidence supports rolling after exercise, though overall evidence is limited. These test different tools with different designs, and the rolling trials could not rule out placebo, so a real difference between the tools isn't established. [@afonso-post-exercise-stretching-2021] [@wiewelhove-foam-rolling-2019]
- Cold water versus the other soreness tools. Cold-water immersion showed clearer short-term soreness and perceived-recovery benefits than the stretching and foam rolling reviews found for those tools, but these are separate reviews with different designs, not head-to-head tests, and neither the cold-water nor the rolling trials could rule out placebo. [@moore-cold-water-immersion-meta-2022] [@afonso-post-exercise-stretching-2021] [@wiewelhove-foam-rolling-2019]
- Cold for soreness versus cold for an injury. The cold-water meta-analysis found less soreness after hard exercise in healthy, uninjured people. The cryotherapy review (abstract only) asks a different question, cold applied to an injured tissue, and finds pain relief only, with animal work hinting it could slow repair. They don't conflict: one is about feeling less sore after training, the other about an injury, where cold is at most pain relief and assessment by a professional comes first. [@moore-cold-water-immersion-meta-2022] [@racinais-cryotherapy-soft-tissue-review-2024]
- Watching markers versus what markers can show. The IOC statement recommends monitoring load and well-being, while the overtraining consensus finds no marker that reliably detects overtraining and the HRV review asks that HRV support decisions, not drive them. These fit together: monitoring describes trends and prompts a conversation, it does not diagnose. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016] [@meeusen-overtraining-consensus-2013] [@dejager-wearable-hrv-menstrual-cycle-2026]

## Women-specific notes
- Several studies (not all) report more sleep disturbance and poorer sleep in the luteal phase (the days before a period); if she notices this, logging sleep alongside her cycle can show whether it applies to her. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Poor sleep after hard sessions may slow glycogen refilling, muscle repair and thinking, according to the ISSN female-athlete stand. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Most sleep studies in the 2014 review either studied men or did not report sex; only a few studies of women are included, so sleep guidance here rests mostly on men and mixed groups. [@halson-sleep-elite-athletes-2014]
- Stretching evidence is mostly from men, with only two trials including both sexes. The foam rolling text read does not report participants' sex. Neither finding has been shown to apply to women runners specifically. [@afonso-post-exercise-stretching-2021] [@wiewelhove-foam-rolling-2019]
- Cold-water immersion evidence is mostly from men: only a handful of the 52 studies included women, there was no analysis by sex, and the authors say the findings may reflect male responses more than female ones. Tell her that when she asks about ice baths. [@moore-cold-water-immersion-meta-2022]
- The sauna study is one of the few here to report women and men separately, and women were the majority of the sauna group (16 of 26). The headline changes (peak core temperature, peak heart rate, VO2max) did not differ by sex. Some details did: the rise in core temperature during the hot run shrank in women but not in men, women showed more active sweat glands on the forearm and men more forearm blood flow, and the run felt easier afterwards for women only. The women's menstrual cycle phase and hormonal contraceptive use varied and were not analysed, and they were about 19 years old, so this is a first look, not settled guidance for women. [@kirby-sauna-sex-differences-runners-2021]
- The cryotherapy review's abstract does not say who was in the few human studies, so it is not women-tested. [@racinais-cryotherapy-soft-tissue-review-2024]
- Hormonal contraceptive users had lower HRV than naturally cycling women, particularly late in the cycle, and combined pill or ring users showed a different pattern from the natural cycle. HRV also tended to be lower after menopause, though age may explain part of that. A lower reading late in her cycle, or on hormonal contraception, can be cycle timing and not poor recovery. [@dejager-wearable-hrv-menstrual-cycle-2026]
- The HRV review is women-only but not athlete-specific, and fewer than half its studies confirmed cycle phase with hormone or ovulation tests. The overtraining consensus abstract doesn't report the sex of the people studied. [@dejager-wearable-hrv-menstrual-cycle-2026] [@meeusen-overtraining-consensus-2013]

## Gaps
- Recovery practices such as massage, compression and active recovery: no source read covers them directly, so the coach should say so rather than recommend them. Cold-water immersion is covered only against passive rest after a single session: no source read covers regular use across a training block or use after long runs specifically.
- Cold on an injury: the cryotherapy review was read at abstract level only (paywalled). Which injuries, which cold methods and how long, and the human studies behind the pain finding are not summarized, and its timing suggestion is the authors' interim view for clinicians, not something tested in people. No source read covers cold on a suspected bone injury or on swelling; those go to a professional (see `health-referrals.md`).
- Sauna: one non-randomized study of young university runners with about half the recruits lost. The control group's figures and the group comparison are in a supplement that was not read, dropouts were not found broken down by group, and no adverse events or safety criteria were found in the text read. The paper's abstract and tables give slightly different figures for some results, so only those that matched or are labelled above are quoted. No source read covers sauna in older or recreational marathon runners, sauna for soreness or recovery, sauna before a hot race, how it interacts with hydration or the menstrual cycle, or sauna with an injury or illness (heat illness warning signs are in `health-referrals.md`).
- Cold-water immersion: the review's total participant count was not found, its counts of studies with women differ between its table and its discussion, and its supplementary files were not read. The stretching review's comparisons with other active recovery methods were not found in the text read.
- Stretching and foam rolling: range-of-motion results from the stretching review and study-level details of the foam rolling review (supplements and forest plots, including confidence intervals and whether the soreness result was statistically significant) were not read. Neither was confirmed to include marathon or distance runners, and neither tests injury.
- How many rest days a week and when to take them: no approved source read gives a number; the plan guardrails in `plan_rules.py` set the structure.
- The IOC load statement was read at abstract level only (paywalled), so its specific monitoring tools and load-change limits are not summarized.
- The Halson review discloses honoraria from the Gatorade Sports Science Institute; much of its evidence is small, older, non-athlete or extreme sleep-deprivation studies.
- Recovery after the marathon itself: not covered by sources read.
- Overtraining: the consensus statement was read at abstract level only (paywalled), so its symptom lists, diagnostic steps and prevention advice are not summarized.
- HRV: no approved source read covers using HRV to guide training, and the review's size-of-change figures could not be reconciled (its abstract and discussion give different ranges). Pregnancy findings were not found in the text read.
