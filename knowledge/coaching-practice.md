# Coaching practice with one runner

## Bottom line for the coach
- How to read the labels below. **Strong** means independent studies agree, **Supported** means some direct evidence but limited, and **Coaching experience** means a practitioner's judgment, which may shape tone and decisions only, never a number used as a rule or a health rule. Shared coaching tradition is not corroboration, so three coaches saying the same thing still counts as coaching experience, and the two Norwegian coach papers count as one line of evidence. Nothing in this section reaches Strong yet: the main review counted results by direction and the autonomy data are correlational. [@talsnes-coaches-underperformance-2025] [@sandbakk-best-practice-coaches-2025] [@norris-running-while-sick]
- **Supported:** Ask how she feels and take the answer seriously. Across 56 studies, self-reported well-being worsened when load rose and improved when it fell, and in 22 of 54 studies it was more sensitive than objective markers. The finding covers self-report in general, not any one questionnaire. [@saw-monitoring-subjective-2016]
- **Supported:** Coaching that gives choices, gives reasons and acknowledges feelings goes with better motivation and well-being, across 131 samples. This is an association from correlational studies, not proof that the coaching causes the benefit. [@mossman-autonomy-support-2022]
- **Supported:** Autonomy does not mean dropping the standard. Setting limits with a reason is part of autonomy-supportive coaching, and the model pairs it with structure and involvement. Hold the plan's guardrails, explain why, and let her choose within them. [@mossman-autonomy-support-2022] [@mageau-vallerand-motivational-model-2003]
- **Supported:** When performance slides or effort stops matching pace, look for the cause before changing the training. World-class coaches reported ruling out medical causes first, then cutting load and working to lower life stress. For the coach, that first step means sending her to a qualified professional, including for illness; no approved source sets rules for training through illness. [@talsnes-coaches-underperformance-2025]
- **Supported:** By message, agree early on how you communicate and what a reply means. Interviews with remote coaches and athletes found trust rested on explicit communication agreements and deliberate effort, and individualizing meant capturing her experience alongside the data. [@stewart-remote-coaching-2026]
- **Coaching experience:** On a flat day, help her sort tired from unmotivated: start gently, see how the body answers, and adjust early rather than fade late. Offer this as a way to decide, with the choice left to her. [@mcmillan-bad-workout-days]
- **Supported:** Raise the menstrual cycle lightly, as an open invitation she can decline. Among elite women skiers, most had talked with a coach about it, but they mostly turned to medical staff, nobody was sure who should start the conversation, and privacy and trust were real barriers. Don't plan around cycle phases. Missed or stopped periods go to a doctor. [@hook-period-communication-2021] [@mcnulty-menstrual-cycle-performance-2020] [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]

## What the sources agree on
- **Supported:** Self-report responds to training load. A systematic review of 56 studies found subjective and objective measures generally did not correlate, and subjective well-being typically worsened after an acute rise in load and with chronic training, and improved after an acute drop. Most objective markers did not respond reliably to acute load changes. The review counted results by direction because studies were too mixed to pool, and its search ended in May 2014. [@saw-monitoring-subjective-2016]
- **Supported:** Keep check-ins brief. The same review says self-report may be used alone or with objective measures, and stresses low burden and short measures so that athletes keep using them. It does not address whether athletes answer honestly. [@saw-monitoring-subjective-2016]
- **Supported:** Autonomy support is linked to motivation and well-being. A meta-analysis (1,320 correlations, 131 samples, 38,844 people) found correlations of .39 with autonomous motivation and -.19 with amotivation, and links with well-being and lower distress. Where it could be tested, there was no evidence of moderation by culture, team versus individual sport, age or competitive standard. Most of the data were cross-sectional, and the authors could not rule out that motivated athletes draw out a more supportive coaching style. [@mossman-autonomy-support-2022]
- **Supported:** Autonomy support has a modest link with results. The association with performance was small (15 samples, 2,843 people, corrected correlation about .21), so promise a better experience of training, not a faster time. [@mossman-autonomy-support-2022]
- **Supported:** What autonomy support looks like: offer choices, give a reason for each task and set limits, acknowledge her feelings and view, give feedback on competence that doesn't try to control her, avoid making her worth depend on results, and avoid rewards or punishments as levers. This list is from the Mageau and Vallerand model as summarized by Mossman; the model itself is theory built from earlier research, not a trial. [@mossman-autonomy-support-2022] [@mageau-vallerand-motivational-model-2003]
- **Supported:** Warning signs elite coaches watch for: declining performance, a mismatch between speed or power and heart rate, lactate or perceived effort, and changes in mood or in how the athlete communicates. Daily follow-up, training diaries, intensity measures and test data were rated the most useful tools. These are 12 male Norwegian coaches of world-class athletes, read from the abstract. [@talsnes-coaches-underperformance-2025]
- **Supported:** Dialogue and ownership. In a multiple case study of 12 male Norwegian coaches, coaches described continuous dialogue used for intensity control and reflection, often involved athletes in planning key sessions, and linked athlete ownership of training to expertise. The authors say this approach depends on a good match between coach and athlete, and their aim was insight and hypotheses, not generalization. Some coaches described seeing athletes in person every day. [@sandbakk-best-practice-coaches-2025]
- **Supported:** Remote coaching is its own setting. In interviews with 10 coaches and 10 athletes, four themes described effective remote endurance coaching: building relationships, individualization, communication and commercial context. Communication was negotiated and set expectations about responsiveness and responsibility, and the authors say remote coaching should not be treated as an extension of face-to-face coaching. [@stewart-remote-coaching-2026]
- **Coaching experience:** Bad days. Treat one off day as information, keep a log to spot patterns, and take repeated flat days as a reason to review the plan rather than as a failure of will. Thresholds and pace adjustments from this article are deliberately left out. [@mcmillan-bad-workout-days]
- **Coaching experience:** Missed sessions, tone. Don't treat missed days as a debt to repay. Pick the plan back up where it stands, and make the first day back easy. Any revised week still has to pass the plan guardrails. [@norris-running-while-sick]
- **Coaching experience:** Illness, tone. Treat the question as a coaching conversation: ask what is going on, lower expectations, and take the pressure of the schedule away. Whether it is safe to run is not the coach's call; refer her to a qualified professional. The article's medical advice, including temperature cutoffs, is deliberately left out. [@norris-running-while-sick]
- **Coaching experience:** Purpose and mindset. Start from where she is now rather than from past results or other runners. Ask why she runs and what she wants from it long term, keep attention on the process, and treat a post-race low as something to explore rather than wait out. Only these communication and mindset ideas are used; the interview's training prescriptions are not. [@roche-skurka-interview-2018]

## Where they differ
- Feel versus data. The review backs self-report and says it may stand alone or sit in a mixed approach, while elite coaches report combining objective and subjective monitoring. These fit: the elite coaches have lab data and daily contact that this coach lacks. Self-report and objective measures generally do not correlate, so one can't stand in for the other. [@saw-monitoring-subjective-2016] [@talsnes-coaches-underperformance-2025]
- In person versus by message. Some Norwegian coaches describe seeing athletes every day and adjusting in person. Remote coaching rests on negotiated, written communication, so elite practice can't simply be copied into a chat. [@sandbakk-best-practice-coaches-2025] [@stewart-remote-coaching-2026]
- Does it depend on the pair? Autonomy support looked similar across sports and competitive standards, yet the Norwegian authors say their dialogue-based approach depends on the match between coach and athlete. One is a pooled correlation and the other a coach-level view, and neither tests the question directly. [@mossman-autonomy-support-2022] [@sandbakk-best-practice-coaches-2025]
- Making up a missed run. Higdon says never skip the long run. The LA Road Runners FAQ allows adding a few miles across later runs, and both warn against cramming close to race day. Norris, writing about illness, says don't make up the mileage. Illness is a different case from a skipped run, and none of these views is tested. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@norris-running-while-sick]

## Women-specific notes
- Most of this evidence is not about a woman like her. The Norwegian coach papers studied elite settings with male coaches only, and the skier study had 6 men among 8 coaches and elite women athletes, so transfer to a recreational woman marathoner is limited. The Sandbakk authors name the lack of female coaches as a limitation. Sex and level in the remote coaching study were not in the abstract read. [@talsnes-coaches-underperformance-2025] [@sandbakk-best-practice-coaches-2025] [@hook-period-communication-2021] [@stewart-remote-coaching-2026]
- **Supported:** The one women-specific study: 13 elite Swedish cross-country skiers (all women) and 8 coaches. 12 of 13 athletes reported talking with a coach about the cycle or contraception in the previous year, yet most said they turned to doctors, nurses or other specialists. All 21 participants wanted to know more. Participants were unsure who should start the conversation. [@hook-period-communication-2021]
- **Supported:** Barriers in that study were knowledge, privacy and discomfort, and the lack of any forum for these talks. Athletes found a female coach easier to talk to, and male coaches described needing more trust first. A coach should expect the same hesitancy, say what it doesn't know, and point to a doctor for medical questions. [@hook-period-communication-2021]
- Autonomy support: the meta-analysis had too few samples to test gender as a moderator. [@mossman-autonomy-support-2022]
- Self-report: the sex of participants was not stated in the text read; participant details are in a supplement that was not read. [@saw-monitoring-subjective-2016]

## Gaps
- Illness: no approved source gives rules for training through illness or returning afterward. Norris is used for tone only, and `health-referrals.md` keeps its illness gap.
- No source tests coaching communication in recreational women marathoners, or by chat or an AI coach.
- Nothing here tests how to push versus hold back; the labels above reflect indirect evidence and practitioner judgment.
- The Talsnes and Sandbakk papers both report 12 male Norwegian coaches and share authors. They are probably the same group, which wasn't confirmed, so they are treated as one line of evidence.
- Read as abstract only: Talsnes, Mageau and Vallerand, and Stewart (through an index page; the journal page was blocked). The Stewart participants' sex and competitive level and the issue details were not seen, and its Crossref record lists no volume or pages.
- Mossman was read from an author copy, by searching the extracted text for key passages rather than line by line.
- Saw: the supplementary study table was not read, and the Saw and Hook reference-list tails were not reviewed.
