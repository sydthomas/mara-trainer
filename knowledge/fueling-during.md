# Fueling during runs

## Bottom line for the coach
- For runs of about an hour or more, start from 30 to 60 g of carbohydrate per hour; that is the women's position stand's realistic starting point. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- For long runs and the marathon, the ceiling from a single sugar is about 60 g per hour; higher rates (toward 90 g per hour, mainly for ultra-endurance) need mixed sugars such as glucose plus fructose, and a marathoner does not necessarily need 90 g per hour. In trained women, 90 g per hour gave no extra benefit over 60 g per hour. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Give carbohydrate targets in grams per hour, not per kg: body size did not predict how much carbohydrate the body can burn from what is eaten. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- Practice the race fueling plan on long runs; the gut adapts to regular carbohydrate intake, and women should check whether rates above 60 g per hour upset their stomach at different points in the cycle. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Caffeine helps endurance performance in women and men alike. Small doses taken during a long effort (roughly 100 to 300 mg, with carbohydrate) are one studied option; larger doses add side effects without extra benefit. Trial it in training, keep it early in the day for sleep, and refer pregnancy questions to a doctor. [@issn-caffeine-2021] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- If Sydney uses caffeine pills or other supplements, suggest third-party certified products (USADA names NSF Certified for Sport). [@usada-supplement-connect]
- Any sign of restriction or guilt around fueling: be supportive, skip numbers, refer to a doctor or registered dietitian. [@acsm-and-dc-nutrition-athletic-performance-2016]

## What the sources agree on
- Carbohydrate need rises with duration: for runs under about an hour little or none is needed; a mouth rinse or small amounts helped about one hour of hard exercise in the studies reviewed; up to about 60 g per hour is advised for 2 to 3 hours; about 90 g per hour for ultra-endurance. Studies were mostly of cyclists, and the review rarely reports sex. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- Intake should be lower at low absolute intensities, which is why a marathon runner may not need the very high rates used by faster or longer-event athletes. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- In a 51-person study of cyclists and triathletes testing 10 to 120 g per hour, performance improved with dose and was best at about 60 to 80 g per hour. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- One sugar (glucose) is burned at up to about 1 g per minute; glucose plus fructose (studied at about 2:1) raised burning to about 1.26 g per minute, up to 75% more than glucose-type sugars alone. In mountain bike racers eating about 95 g per hour, the mixed-sugar drink cut race time 1.8% and reduced stomach cramping. Benefits mostly appeared in efforts of 2.5 hours or more. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- Running and cycling showed similar burning of ingested carbohydrate, so the same advice applies to runners. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- The gut is trainable: 28 days of a high-carbohydrate diet with carbohydrate taken during training raised the amount of ingested carbohydrate endurance athletes could burn. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- Formats: gels and low-fat, low-protein, low-fiber bars were burned at high rates; solid food and very concentrated drinks slow fluid absorption. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- Typical products: a sports gel holds about 25 g of carbohydrate per sachet; sports chews and confectionery about 5 g per piece; sports drinks are 5 to 8% carbohydrate. Some gels also contain caffeine or electrolytes. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- A 6% carbohydrate drink taken during exercise improved endurance in women in both the follicular and luteal phases. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Caffeine: the usual studied dose is 3 to 6 mg per kg (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight), most often about 60 minutes before exercise; the minimum effective dose may be as low as 2 mg/kg (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). Endurance gains are typically about 2 to 4%. [@issn-caffeine-2021] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Caffeine during exercise: benefits seem largest once fatigue builds. Low doses (under about 200 mg) taken before and during exercise with carbohydrate, or 100 to 300 mg taken partway into endurance exercise, improved cycling time trials by about 3 to 7%; low-dose caffeine late in a race matched a larger dose taken before. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@issn-caffeine-2021]
- Forms: gum, mouth rinses, gels, chews, energy drinks and coffee have all been studied; gum is absorbed faster than capsules. Coffee's caffeine content varies widely between shops, which makes dosing imprecise. [@issn-caffeine-2021]
- Side effects (fast heart rate, anxiety, nausea, headache, insomnia) rise with dose and are common at 9 mg/kg or more (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight), a level that adds no benefit. [@issn-caffeine-2021] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Sleep: caffeine's half-life averages 4 to 6 hours (range 1.5 to 10). In one study an evening dose disrupted sleep in 6 of 13 people versus 1 of 13 after a morning dose, so earlier use is advised. [@issn-caffeine-2021]
- Trial caffeine dose, timing and any withdrawal in training before using it in a race; two lab incidents involved huge accidental overdoses from dose calculation errors with pure caffeine. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Supplements, caffeine products included, can be contaminated or mislabeled; if used, choose third-party certified products. [@usada-supplement-connect] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]

## Where they differ
- Upper carbohydrate rate: the review recommends up to about 90 g per hour (mixed sugars) for the longest events and reports a performance peak at 60 to 80 g per hour in mixed-sex or unreported-sex cyclists. The women's position stand starts at 30 to 60 g per hour and cites a study of endurance-trained women (follicular phase) where 60 g per hour gave the most carbohydrate burning and 90 g per hour added nothing. The difference is the population (mostly male cyclists versus trained women) and event length. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Mouth rinsing: the review says rinsing with a carbohydrate drink helped about one hour of hard exercise; the women's position stand says this does not seem to hold for trained female athletes. The review places rinsing only at about one-hour efforts; for longer runs it recommends swallowing carbohydrate. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Caffeine dose: the caffeine position stand and IOC give 3 to 6 mg/kg and call 9 mg/kg unnecessary and side-effect prone; the female-strategies review gives 3 to 9 mg/kg (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). The caffeine stand's range is newer and focused on caffeine, so prefer it. [@issn-caffeine-2021] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Habitual caffeine users: the women's position stand says habitual users may need an acute dose near their usual daily intake; the caffeine stand reports trained cyclists benefited from 3 to 6 mg/kg (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight) regardless of habitual intake or short withdrawal, though one study in low consumers saw the effect shrink after four weeks of daily use. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@issn-caffeine-2021]

## Women-specific notes
- In an Ironman study, women ate slightly less carbohydrate per hour than men, and the link between intake and finish time seen in men was not confirmed in women. Most other carbohydrate-rate research is in men or does not report sex. [@jeukendrup-carbohydrate-during-exercise-2014]
- Women have slower gastric emptying and gut motility than men, and naturally cycling women report more GI distress in the early-follicular and late-luteal phases; tracking cycle day against stomach symptoms on long runs is advised. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- The position stand advises more carbohydrate availability during the luteal phase and during active pill weeks for oral contraceptive users. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Caffeine's endurance benefit does not appear to differ by sex. Hormonal contraceptives and pregnancy slow caffeine clearance. [@issn-caffeine-2021]
- Caffeine builds up more and clears more slowly in the luteal phase for some women, which may worsen premenstrual symptoms, heart rate, anxiety and sleep. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Caffeine has among the strongest supplement evidence in women, alongside iron and creatine. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]

## Gaps
- Fluids and sodium during runs: see hydration.md. Everyday eating: see daily-nutrition.md.
- No approved source gives absolute caffeine safety limits in mg per day or caffeine guidance during pregnancy; the NIH ODS fact sheet that might cover this returned HTTP 403 and could not be read. Pregnancy questions go to a doctor.
- No approved source studies gut training protocols in women or in runners specifically (the gut-training study's participants were described only as endurance athletes).
- The review's carbohydrate-by-duration figure values could not be read from the text; only numbers stated in prose are used here.
- No source covers how to carry or schedule fuel during a marathon (gel spacing, aid-station drinks) beyond product carbohydrate content.
- The ACSM joint position statement is paywalled; only its abstract (no numbers) was read.
