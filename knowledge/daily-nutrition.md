# Daily nutrition for training

## Bottom line for the coach
- Eating enough overall comes first. For women, the main nutrition priority is adequate energy intake and energy availability (the energy left for the body after training), with attention to meal timing around runs. Talk about it as a concept; never give calorie numbers. Depth on low energy availability and RED-S: see women.md. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Carbohydrate needs rise with training load, and most athletes, women especially, tend to eat too little of it. Women should meet carbohydrate needs in every cycle phase first, with a bit more in the luteal phase and in active-pill weeks of oral contraceptives. Run-specific fueling: see fueling-before.md, fueling-during.md and fueling-after.md. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Protein: spread it across the day, a serving every 3 to 4 hours, with moderate servings (about 20 to 30 g) favored over one big or many tiny ones, and a quality protein source near hard sessions. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@mercer-protein-female-athletes-2020]
- Food first: build meals on whole, nutrient-dense foods (protein foods, dairy, vegetables, fruits, healthy fats, whole grains), choose water or unsweetened drinks, and limit highly processed foods and added sugars. [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030]
- Iron matters for women runners: deficiency is far more common in women and can slow endurance performance. Suggest a blood test with a doctor; don't suggest high-dose iron unless a deficiency has been confirmed. Depth: see women.md. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Supplements play a small role at most. Any supplement should follow a professional assessment, and if Sydney uses one, suggest a third-party certified product (USADA names NSF Certified for Sport); no product is guaranteed risk-free. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@usada-supplement-connect]
- For a personalized eating plan, refer to a registered dietitian (in the US and Canada a Certified Specialist in Sports Dietetics, CSSD, is a sports-nutrition credential); the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics runs a directory of credentialed practitioners. Any sign of restriction, weight-loss pressure or guilt around food: be supportive, skip numbers, and refer to a doctor or registered dietitian. [@acsm-and-dc-nutrition-athletic-performance-2016] [@eatright-sports-athletic-performance]

## What the sources agree on
- Daily carbohydrate for active people trying to perform well is suggested at 6 to 10 g/kg/day (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight); the review notes that most athletes undereat calories and carbohydrate in particular. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Daily protein for women athletes sits in the middle-to-upper part of the sports range. The women's position stand gives 1.4 to 2.2 g/kg/day for all menstrual stages, spread through the day (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- A systematic review of 14 studies (204 recreationally active or competitive premenopausal women) estimated endurance athletes' average requirement at 1.28 to 1.63 g/kg/day and a level covering nearly everyone at 1.59 to 2.02 g/kg/day (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). The endurance figures came from three nitrogen-balance studies done in the mid-follicular phase or in hormonal-contraceptive users. Needs were similar for endurance, resistance and intermittent sport. [@mercer-protein-female-athletes-2020]
- Protein doses of about 0.32 to 0.38 g/kg before or after exercise gave good responses, mostly in resistance and intermittent-sport studies; endurance evidence for single doses is thin (one study) (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). [@mercer-protein-female-athletes-2020] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- The protein studies mostly used high-quality animal proteins (whey, egg), so people eating mainly plant protein may need more and should vary their plant sources. [@mercer-protein-female-athletes-2020] [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030]
- Fats: get them mostly from whole foods (fish rich in omega-3s, nuts, seeds, olives, avocados, dairy, eggs, meats), cook with oils such as olive oil, and keep saturated fat under about a tenth of daily energy. A women-focused review suggests at least about a fifth of energy from fat. [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Iron: women need more than men; the recommended intake for women is 18 mg/day versus 8 mg/day for men, and athletes struggling to keep iron status up may need more than that. Diagnosis uses several blood markers together (such as ferritin and transferrin saturation), and high-dose iron belongs only to confirmed deficiency. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- In 23 studies of 669 high-level female athletes (ages 13 to 47, 16 sports), iron-deficient women were slower in endurance time trials (iron-sufficient athletes 1 to 19% faster), and medically supervised supplementation improved endurance measures; the clearest gains came with about 100 mg of elemental iron daily by mouth for up to 8 weeks, or injections. Side effects (often stomach upset) were common. These were high-level athletes, so apply cautiously to a recreational runner. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- Unneeded iron supplements can cause vomiting, diarrhea and stomach pain and, over time, iron overload and liver damage. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Vitamin D: there is no agreed blood level that defines deficiency; general maintenance intakes of 800 to 2000 IU/day are cited, and higher doses for a diagnosed deficiency need monitoring by a professional. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Calcium: the daily recommendation for adult women is 1000 mg; women athletes tend to eat less calcium than men, and runners who avoid dairy or undereat are at higher risk of falling short. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Vegetarian and vegan eaters should watch for gaps in iron, vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium and iodine (vegans more broadly), monitor status periodically, and use targeted supplements where needed. [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Supplement contamination is real: in one survey about 15% of over 600 products contained undeclared steroid precursors, and even ordinary vitamins and minerals have occasionally contained banned substances. In the US, supplements are not reviewed by the FDA before sale. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@usada-supplement-connect]
- Third-party certification lowers but does not remove the risk; USADA recommends products certified by a program that tests for substances banned in sport and names NSF Certified for Sport. Supplements were linked to about 23,000 US emergency visits a year (2015 data). [@usada-supplement-connect] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Supplements with good performance evidence (caffeine, creatine, nitrate, beta-alanine, bicarbonate) have specific doses and side effects; caffeine use around runs: see fueling-during.md. Creatine's usual maintenance dose is 3 to 5 g/day, the same for women and men. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]

## Where they differ
- Protein ranges vary by population and method: the general dietary guidelines give 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg/day for the public, the women's position stand 1.4 to 2.2, the systematic review's near-universal level 1.59 to 2.02, and a women-focused review at least 1.6 as a starting point (all per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). The review warns its nitrogen-balance and tracer methods may overestimate needs. [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@mercer-protein-female-athletes-2020] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Carbohydrate framing differs: the general dietary guidelines push to cut refined carbohydrates and mention lower-carbohydrate diets for some chronic diseases, while the sports sources stress that athletes, especially women, usually eat too little carbohydrate. The difference is the audience (general public vs people in heavy training). [@dietary-guidelines-americans-2025-2030] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Vitamin D amounts differ: one source cites a 600 IU/day recommended intake for adults up to 70 and calls 2000 to 4000 IU safe, the IOC cites 800 to 2000 IU for maintenance and says there is no consensus on target blood levels. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Calcium: 1000 mg/day for adult women in general, but the IOC suggests 1500 mg/day plus 1500 to 2000 IU vitamin D for athletes with low energy availability or menstrual dysfunction. That is a medical situation; refer rather than advise. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Iron-deficiency cutoffs vary: the systematic review included women with ferritin under 40 ug/L, most included studies used under 20, and its staging scheme starts at under 30. Blood results should be read by a doctor. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- Caffeine dose: one review lists 3 to 9 mg/kg before exercise, while the IOC gives 3 to 6 mg/kg and says 9 mg/kg or more adds no benefit and more side effects (per kg body mass — the coach doesn't ask for or compute with body weight). [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]
- Who uses supplements most: the IOC reports athlete surveys showing higher use in men, while the women's position stand says use is highest among women (65.4% of college women athletes in one study used supplements at least monthly). Surveys differ in definitions and methods. [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]

## Women-specific notes
- In the luteal phase (after ovulation) women should aim for the upper end of protein needs, roughly 12% more, and a bit more carbohydrate; the same applies to active-pill weeks on oral contraceptives for carbohydrate. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- The systematic review could not tell whether cycle phase or contraception changes protein needs: there were too few comparisons. [@mercer-protein-female-athletes-2020]
- Iron deficiency is 5 to 7 times more common in women athletes than men; prevalence ranges from 9 to 60% in women versus up to about 30% in men, and menstruation can cost up to about 10 mg of iron per cycle. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- Hormonal contraception appears protective against iron deficiency, and women on the pill should not take iron supplements without medical advice. Iron may absorb better in the follicular phase than the luteal phase, though this needs more research. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Women who are vegan, vegetarian or undereating are at particular risk of low iron. [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Caffeine clears more slowly in the luteal phase and may worsen premenstrual symptoms, anxiety and sleep then. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@wohlgemuth-female-nutrition-strategies-2021]
- Evidence base: the protein and iron findings come from women-only studies (204 and 669 women); the IOC supplement statement rarely reports sex in the trials it draws on, so its performance-supplement findings may rest mostly on men. [@mercer-protein-female-athletes-2020] [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025] [@ioc-dietary-supplements-2018]

## Gaps
- The ACSM/Academy/Dietitians of Canada joint position stand (2016) is paywalled; only its abstract was read, which has no numbers. Its daily carbohydrate, protein, fat and micronutrient guidance is not in this section.
- No approved source read here gives a tiered daily carbohydrate range by training load (light vs moderate vs heavy days); only the single 6 to 10 g/kg/day range above was found.
- The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements fact sheet (nih-ods-exercise-athletic-performance) returned an access error (403) and was not read.
- The eatright.org page lists articles and the practitioner directory but gave no specific daily-eating guidance.
- Energy availability thresholds exist in the sources but are expressed in calories, so they are left out by design; RED-S and the Female Athlete Triad (ioc-reds-consensus-2023, female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2) belong in women.md and were not audited here.
- Sims, ROAR (sims-roar-revised-2024) is a book and was not read; cite by reference only if ever used.
- Little women-specific evidence covers recreational (not high-level) runners, plant-based athletes, or how daily fat intake affects endurance training.
