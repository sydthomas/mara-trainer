# Women runners

## Bottom line for the coach
- Do not plan training around cycle phases. Across 78 studies of naturally menstruating women, performance was at most trivially lower in the early follicular phase (around the period), the evidence was rated low quality, and the authors call for an individual approach rather than general rules. [@mcnulty-menstrual-cycle-performance-2020]
- If the runner wants to, she can log her cycle (or her contraceptive pattern) next to training and recovery to spot her own patterns; that is what the female-athlete nutrition position stand suggests. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023] [@mcnulty-menstrual-cycle-performance-2020]
- The pill is not a performance decision. Pill users may perform very slightly worse on average than naturally menstruating women, but the effect is most likely trivial, and performance looked the same on pill days and break days. No blanket advice either way. [@elliott-sale-oral-contraceptives-performance-2020]
- Missed or stopped periods are a health signal, not a sign of fitness. Changes in periods, a past bone stress injury, food restriction or worry about weight mean: be supportive, skip numbers, and suggest a doctor (and a sports dietitian). [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2] [@ioc-reds-consensus-2023]
- Iron deficiency is common in women athletes and can cost a few percent of endurance performance. If she reports unusual fatigue or falling performance, suggest a blood test with her doctor; any iron supplement belongs under medical supervision, so the coach gives no doses. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- Women adapt to heat training clearly (lower heart rate and core temperature, more sweat, better performance in heat), so a warm race is something to prepare for gradually, not avoid. [@kelly-heat-adaptation-females-2023]
- After childbirth, return to running should go through a pelvic health physiotherapist, who checks pelvic floor and abdominal wall function and readiness for impact. [@goom-postnatal-return-to-running-2019]
- Say plainly when advice rests on studies of men: in sport science papers from 2014 to 2020, only 34% of participants were women and only 6% of papers studied women alone. [@cowley-invisible-sportswomen-2021]

## What the sources agree on
- Menstrual cycle and performance. A Bayesian meta-analysis (78 studies, 1,193 naturally menstruating women; 51 studies with 709 women in the main pairwise analysis) found a trivial average drop in the early follicular phase versus other phases, and differences between studies were large. When only high or moderate quality studies were kept (16 studies) the drop shrank to near zero. Only 8% of studies were rated high quality, and only 59% checked cycle phase with blood hormone levels. [@mcnulty-menstrual-cycle-performance-2020]
- Why cycle studies disagree. A methods guide from women's-health researchers says cycle phases are defined by hormones, not calendar days. A regular period does not prove ovulation happened, calendar counting needs to be backed up by ovulation tests or hormone measures, and studies that skipped this lumped different hormone profiles together and produced conflicting results. [@elliott-sale-methods-women-research-2021] [@mcnulty-menstrual-cycle-performance-2020]
- Oral contraceptives. A meta-analysis of 42 studies (590 women) found pill users may perform slightly worse than naturally menstruating women on average, but the group effect is most likely trivial and varies a lot between studies; performance was steady across the pill cycle. 83% of studies were moderate, low or very low quality. The authors advise against blanket guidance for or against the pill and against separate plans for pill and break days. [@elliott-sale-oral-contraceptives-performance-2020]
- Contraceptive types differ. Pills, implants, injections, hormonal IUDs, rings and patches all change a woman's own hormones (copper IUDs do not), and pills differ in formulation, so a finding for one product should not be assumed for all. [@elliott-sale-methods-women-research-2021] [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Energy deficiency: the Triad. The Female Athlete Triad links three areas: energy status, reproductive function and bone health. It starts with not eating enough for the training done (with or without disordered eating), and each area runs on a spectrum from healthy to subclinical to disease. The 2025 update adds bone stress injuries to the bone spectrum and no longer uses a single energy-availability cutoff, because period problems show up above it in some women and it is hard to measure in daily life. [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Energy deficiency: REDs. The IOC describes Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport as health and performance problems in women and men when energy intake is too low for training, and its 2023 update adds low carbohydrate availability and mental health as growing concerns, plus a clinical assessment tool for clinicians. [@ioc-reds-consensus-2023]
- What low energy can harm. The female-athlete nutrition stand lists reproductive, bone, heart, gut, hormone and nerve effects, plus more fatigue, illness, injury and poorer response to training; it notes low energy can be unintentional, especially in heavy training. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Warning signs a coach can notice (screening topics, not a diagnosis): changes in or loss of periods, fewer periods in a year, a past stress fracture or bone injury that cost training time, special diets or avoiding foods, concern about weight, compulsive exercise and undereating without meaning to. [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Who helps. For women at moderate or high Triad risk the consensus recommends a team of a physician, a registered (ideally sports) dietitian and a mental health professional, and it advises against a weight-focused approach. The first step in care is reversing the energy deficit (eating more and/or training less), decided by that team. [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Bone stress injuries. Across 38 prospective studies (35,689 runners, 40.8% women), overall injury rates were the same for women and men, but women had about twice the risk of bone stress injury; the authors link this to the Triad and REDs, hormones, biomechanics and calcium and vitamin D intake. [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021] In 45 premenopausal women runners, those with injuries at high-risk sites (pelvis, sacrum, femoral neck) had lower spine bone density, more shape and eating concerns and more short sleep than those with shin or foot injuries; running load rates did not tell the groups apart. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Iron. A systematic review (23 studies, 669 high-level women athletes aged 13 to 47) found iron deficiency in 9 to 60% of women athletes across studies, linked it to roughly 3 to 4% worse endurance performance, and found that supervised supplementation in deficient athletes improved endurance and related measures. Reasons women are at risk include menstrual blood loss, diet and heavy training. The authors advise athletes with deficiency to see a sports medicine doctor or sports dietitian. Overall study quality was moderate and samples were small. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- Heat. In 30 studies (235 women, mostly heat-chamber protocols), heat training lowered core temperature during exercise by about 0.4 °C and heart rate by about 14 beats per minute, raised sweat rate by about 30%, and improved performance in the heat. Physical adaptations were most consistent after about 8 to 14 days of training in heat, while performance improved after short, medium and long programs alike. [@kelly-heat-adaptation-females-2023]

## Where they differ
- Should training or eating change with the cycle? The two meta-analyses on performance say no general rules, because effects are trivial and the evidence is low quality. [@mcnulty-menstrual-cycle-performance-2020] [@elliott-sale-oral-contraceptives-performance-2020] The ISSN female-athlete stand goes further and suggests phase-specific nutrition tweaks (for example more attention to carbohydrate and protein in the luteal phase), while calling its own evidence limited. The difference is in what was measured: the meta-analyses looked at performance outcomes; the stand draws on metabolism studies and expert judgment. Treat cycle-based nutrition tweaks as optional and low confidence. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Triad versus REDs. The Triad Coalition says REDs is a broader model that adds many outcomes with weaker causal evidence, that the Triad components have the strongest evidence, and that the 2023 REDs update dropped mention of the Triad. [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2] The IOC frames REDs as affecting both sexes and separates problematic from adaptive low energy availability. [@ioc-reds-consensus-2023] For the coach this does not change anything: both point to the same warning signs and the same referral.
- Iron cutoffs. Studies in the iron review defined deficiency with ferritin cutoffs from under 12 to under 35 µg/L (most often under 20), while the review itself used under 40; there is no consensus, and ferritin rises with inflammation. Interpreting blood results is the doctor's job. [@pengelly-iron-deficiency-female-athletes-2025]
- Load versus bone and energy. Load is often blamed for bone stress injuries, but in the women-only study, running load rates did not distinguish injury history while bone density, eating attitudes and sleep did. It was small (15 per group) and cross-sectional, so it shows associations, not causes. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]

## Women-specific notes
- Pill and other hormonal contraceptive users: a withdrawal bleed is not a period, and hormonal contraception hides whether her own cycle is normal. The Triad consensus recommends against using the combined pill to restart periods or protect bone in energy-deficient athletes, because it masks the problem. [@elliott-sale-methods-women-research-2021] [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2] The ISSN stand also notes the pill may mask early signs of low energy availability. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Recovery of periods after an energy deficit does not by itself mean ovulation and hormones are back to normal; several regular cycles are a better sign. This is for the runner's doctor to judge. [@female-athlete-triad-coalition-2025-update-part2]
- Women with a past bone stress injury: a history of bone stress injury is among the strongest risk factors for another, and the study authors recommend a full risk review (nutrition, periods, sleep, bone density) after any bone stress injury. [@tenforde-bone-stress-female-runners-2024]
- Luteal phase: the ISSN stand reports more sleep disturbance in the luteal phase in several studies (not all), and says hormones in that phase raise hyponatremia risk. Treat these as things to watch, not rules. [@issn-female-athlete-nutrition-2023]
- Postpartum runners: the guideline by a running physiotherapist and two pelvic health physiotherapists covers signs of pelvic floor and abdominal wall dysfunction, risk factors, and strength, impact and readiness assessments before running, and names breastfeeding, sleep and psychosocial factors as key considerations. [@goom-postnatal-return-to-running-2019]
- Heat studies in women did not control cycle phase, so whether adaptation differs by phase is unknown. [@kelly-heat-adaptation-females-2023]

## Gaps
- No approved source read here gives an evidence-based return-to-running timeline after childbirth; the Goom guideline's details sit behind a sign-up form, so only its public summary page was read. Postpartum questions go to a pelvic health physiotherapist.
- The IOC REDs statement, the ACSM fluid statement and the Cowley audit were read at abstract level only; the REDs warning-sign list and assessment tool details are not summarized here.
- The Triad 2025 Part 2 statement is very long; its screening, treatment, contraception and return-to-play parts were read, but not every section.
- Perimenopause and menopause, pregnancy, endometriosis and PCOS are not covered by sources read for this section.
- Most sources read do not report results separately for recreational women marathoners; most study samples were high-level or mixed athletes.
- Sex differences in heat-illness risk: not covered by any source read.
