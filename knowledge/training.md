# Marathon training

## Bottom line for the coach
- Keep most running easy. Easy means a relaxed, conversational pace (LA Road Runners call it about 6/10 effort and put 80 to 90% of training there; Chicago's recovery pace is 4 to 5/10). *80/20 Running* argues for about 80% of runs at low intensity and 20% at high intensity. [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@fitzgerald-80-20-running]
- A first-marathon week in the published plans is 4 or 5 runs: one long run, at most one harder session, the rest easy, plus rest or cross-training days. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- Build the long run gradually with regular step-back weeks. Novice plans peak at 16 to 20 mi, three to four weeks before race day. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- Avoid sudden jumps in training load. The IOC consensus names poor load management as a major injury risk factor, including rapid load changes, crowded schedules, travel and psychological stress. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]
- Set workout paces only from a recent race or time trial. Chicago wants a race from the last two months, and LA Road Runners a race or a 1 to 2 mile time trial. Without one, coach by effort. [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- Plans are guides, not rules. Don't try to make up a missed long run close to race day. Stop running when actually hurt (not just sore) and send her to a qualified professional. [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- Women's overall running-injury risk is about the same as men's, but bone stress injuries are roughly twice as common in women. Midfoot, shin, hip or other bone pain that persists goes to a clinician. Energy availability topics go to `women.md` and `health-referrals.md`. [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021]

## What the sources agree on
- **Plan length and phases.** Published plans run 18 weeks (Chicago, Hal Higdon Novice 1), 20 weeks (Boston) or 25 weeks (LA Road Runners). Chicago calls 18 weeks ideal and advises against any plan shorter than 12 weeks. Boston's 20 weeks split into a 3-week prep phase, 6 weeks of building, 9 marathon-specific weeks and a 2-week taper. [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **Run days.** Higdon Novice 1 has 4 runs a week (easy Tuesday and Thursday, a midweek "sorta-long" run on Wednesday, the long run on Saturday) plus one cross-training day and two rest days. Chicago has 5 runs a week (three recovery runs, one speed run, one long run) and two rest days. LA Road Runners Level 2 has 4 or 5 runs with Tuesday and Friday off. Boston Level One is about 4 run days, and its higher levels go up to 6 or 7. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- **Weekly volume for beginners.** Higdon Novice 1 starts at 15 mi a week and peaks at about 40 mi in week 15. Boston Level One starts at 25 mi and peaks at 40 mi. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- **Long-run progression.** Higdon builds from 6 mi to a 20 mi peak three weeks out, with a step-back week every third week (weeks 3, 6, 9, 12). LA Road Runners Level 2 builds from 3 mi to 20 mi four weeks out, cutting back about every three to five weeks. Chicago builds from 5 mi through 25K (about 15.5 mi) and 30K (about 18.6 mi) to 20 mi four weeks out. Boston Level One peaks at 16 to 18 mi. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- **Long-run pace.** Long runs are run easy. Higdon says she should be able to talk, roughly 30 to 90 seconds per mile slower than marathon pace, and adds that a first-timer can't know marathon pace yet, so finishing matters more than speed. LA Road Runners label long runs "easy". Chicago asks for a comfortable progression run that starts slower, finishes faster and averages recovery pace. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan]
- **A tune-up half marathon.** Higdon suggests racing a half marathon in week 8 and using the result in a pace calculator to estimate marathon pace. LA Road Runners schedule the Rose Bowl Half Marathon eight weeks before the LA Marathon. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **Workout types.** Harder sessions are tempo or threshold runs, intervals, fartlek and hills. Boston's higher levels add marathon-pace blocks and long-run "simulations" on rolling courses. Chicago says to run hill workouts by effort, not pace. [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **Rest and flexibility.** Every plan builds in rest days. Chicago says the best run is sometimes no run, to keep speed and long runs spaced apart, and to adjust the plan to the runner's life. Higdon allows an occasional skipped workout but not a skipped long run, and says consistency over the whole program matters most. Boston calls its plans guides, not prescriptions. [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- **Effort over the watch.** Chicago treats each pace target as the middle of a range and says pace shifts with weather, elevation, stress, fatigue and poor sleep, so effort is the better guide when they disagree. [@chicago-marathon-training-plan]
- **Walking is allowed.** Higdon accepts walk breaks in training and in the race, ideally taken by choice (for example through aid stations) before fatigue forces them. LA Road Runners Level 1 is aimed at walkers and run/walkers. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **Load and injury.** An IOC expert group reviewed how training and competition load relates to injury and concluded that poor load management is a major risk factor. Its guidance covers prescribing and monitoring load and athlete well-being. Only the abstract was read, so no specific progression numbers are taken from it. [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]
- **Periodized planning is the standard.** The RRCA's Level 2 coach course trains coaches to build periodized plans that manage fatigue and bring athletes to a peak at the right time. Its Level 1 course covers program design, workout types, injury prevention and environmental factors. Its public page gives no specific numbers. [@rrca-coaching-certification]

## Where they differ
- **Speed work for a first marathon.** Higdon Novice 1 has no speed work at all: every run is easy. Chicago has one speed run every week from week 1 (short intervals at 5K pace in week 1). LA Road Runners size speed work as a share of weekly mileage: 5% at Level 1, 10% at Level 2, up to 20% at Level 4. Why: Higdon's novice plan aims only to finish. Chicago and LA Road Runners serve every level from one template and scale intensity by level. For a first-timer the safest common ground is no more than one quality session a week, kept small. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **How long the longest run is, and when.** Higdon and Chicago peak at 20 mi; Boston Level One stops at 16 to 18 mi. Higdon peaks three weeks out, Chicago and LA Road Runners four weeks out. Chicago also schedules a 99-minute run three weeks out, and LA Road Runners an 18-miler six weeks out, so their last big efforts are spread over more weeks. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- **Long run versus weekly total.** Higdon says never skip the long run. The LA Road Runners FAQ says total weekly volume matters more than any single long run, and that a missed long run should be made up by adding a few miles across later runs, never all in one. Both agree not to cram missed miles close to race day. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **What "easy" and "tempo" mean.** Chicago rates recovery pace at 4 to 5/10 and tempo at 6/10 (about 30 to 35 seconds per mile slower than 5K pace). LA Road Runners rate easy at 6/10 and threshold at 8/10 ("comfortably uncomfortable", between 5K and 10K pace). The same workout name can mean different efforts across plans, so translate by description, not label. Note that Chicago's printed definition of recovery pace repeats its tempo text, which looks like a layout error. [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **Long runs at marathon pace.** The novice plans keep long runs easy. Boston's higher levels put marathon-pace blocks into long runs, and LA Road Runners add 20 to 40 minute marathon-pace runs on Sundays in the second half of the plan. Why: marathon-specific work is aimed at runners with a time goal and a known race pace. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **Strength training.** Higdon tells runners who have never lifted not to start during the program. Chicago and LA Road Runners schedule a strength session most weeks. Why: Higdon is minimizing new stress for beginners, while the race-organizer plans treat strength as part of the routine. See `strength.md` for the evidence. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans]
- **Fixed plan or self-built plan.** The race-organizer plans give a fixed schedule to adjust. *Run Faster* (Hudson and Fitzgerald) teaches runners to assess themselves and design their own program, according to its publisher's page. Both approaches want the plan fitted to the runner. [@hudson-run-faster-5k-marathon] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan]
- **Strength of evidence.** The intensity split (80/20 or 80 to 90% easy) comes from a book's publisher page and a coached program's guidance, not from a study an approved source summarizes here. The IOC load statement is expert consensus. Treat the plan details as common practice, not tested rules. [@fitzgerald-80-20-running] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@ioc-load-injury-risk-2016]

## Women-specific notes
- None of the published plans read here differ by sex. They are general plans by level, not built or tested on women. [@hal-higdon-marathon-training] [@chicago-marathon-training-plan] [@la-marathon-larr-training-plans] [@boston-marathon-baa-training-plans]
- Injury rates by sex (38 prospective studies, 35,689 runners, 40.8% female): overall injury rates were about the same (20.8 per 100 women and 20.4 per 100 men; risk ratio 0.99). Bone stress injuries were about twice as common in women (risk ratio for men 0.52, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.76, from 4 to 5 studies). Achilles tendon problems were more common in men (risk ratio 1.86, 2 studies). [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021]
- In that review, women had slightly higher injury risk in studies of races up to 10 km. For longer distances the difference was not significant. The paper is internally inconsistent on which way that longer-distance trend points, and training load was too poorly reported to analyze. [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021]
- The authors suggest preventing bone stress injuries by screening for low energy availability and low bone density, and they discuss hormonal status, biomechanics, calcium and vitamin D as possible factors. The coach doesn't screen. It refers persistent bone pain or energy-availability worries to a professional (see `health-referrals.md` and `women.md`). [@hollander-sex-differences-running-injuries-2021]
- Most sport-science evidence is about men. An audit of 5,261 papers (2014 to 2020, about 12.5 million participants) found 34% of participants were female and only 6% of papers studied women alone. Training findings from male samples may not transfer, so say so when that's all there is. [@cowley-invisible-sportswomen-2021]
- Methods guidance for research on women says male-derived findings apply poorly to women because of sex differences in reproductive hormones. When judging a study, check whether it reports cycle phase and hormonal contraceptive use. [@elliott-sale-methods-women-research-2021]
- Menstrual cycle phase: across 78 studies of naturally menstruating women, performance was at most trivially lower in the early follicular phase (around the period), and the evidence was low quality. The authors advise against blanket cycle-based training rules and favor tracking how she personally responds. Details are in `women.md`. [@mcnulty-menstrual-cycle-performance-2020]

## Gaps
- No approved source read for this section gives a tested rule for how fast weekly mileage or long-run distance may rise. The IOC consensus full text (which may contain such guidance) is paywalled and only its abstract was read. The "10% rule" is not supported by anything read here.
- No approved peer-reviewed study of intensity distribution (polarized versus threshold training) was read. The 80/20 claim rests on a book's publisher page and the LA Road Runners guidance.
- *Daniels' Running Formula* and *Advanced Marathoning* (Pfitzinger and Douglas): the publisher pages returned rate-limit errors (HTTP 429) during the audit, so nothing is taken from them.
- Venice Run Club: the page returned HTTP 429 and wasn't read.
- London Marathon and Marathon Handbook plans are marked not fetchable in the registry and were not read.
- No approved source compares training plans or responses for women versus men, and none covers training around pregnancy, postpartum or menopause here (see `women.md`).
- Strength and cross-training evidence lives in `strength.md` and `cross-training.md`, not here.
